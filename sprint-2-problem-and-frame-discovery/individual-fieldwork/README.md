# Assignment 1 Submission: ANC Meetings and the Impact Gap

**Upskiller:** [Name]
**Friction focus:** Sustained civic action at the ANC level
**Submitted to:** individual-fieldwork/

### What I looked at

I focused on Advisory Neighborhood Commission meetings. ANCs are the most local piece of DC government. Commissioners are unpaid, elected from small single-member districts, and meet monthly to advise city agencies on liquor licenses, zoning, traffic, and neighborhood issues. Anyone can show up and speak. In principle, this is the point of entry to civic action in DC. In practice, most residents never attend one.

I wanted to understand what happens to the people who do show up. Not the ones who don't know ANCs exist. The ones who found their way to a meeting, participated, and then had to decide whether to come back.

### What I observed

I attended two ANC meetings in August (ANC 6B in person, ANC 1A on Zoom), talked to four people who have attended at least one ANC meeting in the last year, and read the last six months of meeting minutes for both commissions.

A few things stood out:

- **First-time attendees show up for one specific issue.** A new bar's liquor license. A curb cut. A proposed development. They come, they testify, they leave. Of the four people I interviewed, three had attended exactly one ANC meeting in their lives.
- **The one who came back was angry.** She had testified against a zoning variance, watched her ANC vote her way, and then watched the Board of Zoning Adjustment approve it anyway six weeks later. She now attends, in her words, "to make it harder for them to pretend nobody objected."
- **Meetings are long, procedural, and mostly not about you.** A three-hour meeting might have twenty minutes on the thing that brought a resident in. The rest is motions, seconds, reports from city agencies, and business the resident has no context for.
- **The feedback loop is broken by design.** ANCs advise. Agencies decide. DC law says agencies must give ANC positions "great weight," but great weight is a procedural nod, not a binding constraint. Residents rarely learn this before they participate. Many learn it the way my interviewee did: by watching their input lose.

### What's been tried

The visible attempts to fix ANC engagement have almost all been about information and access:

- Better ANC websites and unified dc.gov pages
- Email newsletters from individual commissioners
- Hybrid Zoom meetings post-2020, so you don't have to sit in a church basement on a weeknight
- Neighborhood listservs and social media accounts that translate agendas into plain English
- Civic tech projects that summarize meetings or track votes

These have mostly worked at what they were built to do. It's easier to find your ANC meeting than it was ten years ago. Hybrid attendance is higher than pre-2020 in-person attendance.

What none of them fixed is what happens after you participate. The information-and-access layer is better. The impact layer is the same.

### My hypothesis

Past attempts failed because they solved the wrong side of the effort-vs-impact equation. Everyone assumed the problem was that people couldn't find or reach the meetings. The real friction is that reaching the meeting and participating well doesn't reliably produce the outcome participation is supposed to produce.

The structure that gives ANCs their legitimacy (close to residents, low-stakes enough that unpaid volunteers can run them) is the same structure that limits their power (they advise, they don't decide). Give ANCs binding authority and they become another layer of city government with elections, budgets, staff, and the same distance from residents that DC Council already has. Keep them advisory and residents keep learning the hard way that showing up doesn't change the outcome.

The people who keep coming back are the ones who reframed why they show up. They aren't there to win. They're there to make a record, build relationships with commissioners, or keep a running case against the agency. That's a different theory of civic action than "participate and your voice will be heard," and nobody teaches it to first-timers.
