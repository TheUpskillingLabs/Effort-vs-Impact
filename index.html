Effort v. Impact

Why showing up stopped feeling worth it

Some people would engage — if they believed a given civic action was worth the effort. The barriers are too high and the incentives are too low: it takes real effort just to find out what's available and whether it matters — and when the feedback loop is broken, people stop trying because they never see the result of their effort.

The Paradox

Why This Stays Stuck

Two forces work together to keep people out — and each one makes the other worse.

Force 1

It's rational to opt out

When it takes real work to find out what to do — and there's no way to tell if it worked — stepping back is the sensible choice. That's not laziness. It's people making a reasonable call with limited time and no information.

Force 2

It only works if we all show up

Civic engagement pays off when enough people take part — one vote or one comment rarely moves anything on its own. So everyone waits for someone else to go first, and nothing moves.

Why it sticks around: this isn't about people who don't care — it's a data problem. Most people don't know where to act, what it will take, or whether it worked, and some officials benefit from keeping it that way.

What's actually going on: this mostly isn't a story about people trying to keep the public out — most civic leaders want more engagement and are stuck in the same broken loop. What they're missing isn't the will, it's the marketing budget and reach to get the word out.

What We Found

The Themes Beneath It

Everything in the evidence map below points at two missing pieces of information.

Theme

What's available

People need to know what civic actions exist and what each one would actually be trying to change. At the local level, that information mostly doesn't exist — so there's nothing to weigh the effort against.

Theme

Whether it worked

Even when someone acts, there's no feedback loop telling them whether it changed anything. Without that signal, there's no way to judge the action worth repeating — or worth trying at all.

How We Know

The Research Backs This Up

This isn't just our read of the room — three separate bodies of research point the same direction.

Political science

  • Communities solve shared problems on their own — but only when people can see the rules, the effort involved, and whether it's working. (Elinor Ostrom, Nobel Prize–winning research on collective action)
  • When people believe the usual channels won't respond, they don't disappear — they redirect into protests, boycotts, and action outside the system. The impulse to act doesn't go away; it just moves.

Public opinion

  • Pew: trust in government sits near a 70-year low, with a sharp partisan split — this is the dominant public mood, not a fringe complaint.
  • Pew (2026): the most civically active people are also among the most anxious about whether any of it matters — engagement and doubt travel together, not apart.

Case studies

  • vTaiwan invites citizens, experts, and officials to work through a specific issue online, surfaces the ideas that get broad agreement, and commits the government to act on it — turning public consensus into real national policy, including new ride-sharing rules that ended a years-long standoff with taxi drivers.
  • Porto Alegre, Brazil lets residents vote directly on city spending — and those votes become real line items in the budget, not a suggestion box nobody reads.

Voices we still need

The open questions in each area before we commit to a frame.

Political science

  • Are people disengaging because they've lost trust, or because they don't have enough information to act?
  • Does low trust drive people to disengage, or does disengaging drive the low trust — which comes first?
  • Does this pattern hold outside the U.S., or is it shaped by how our specific system works (district lines, election rules)?
  • If the payoff became clearer, would more people get involved — or would they just redirect their energy elsewhere?

Public opinion

  • Do people distrust government because they can't see their impact, or because they've lost on the merits and know it?
  • Is the "engaged but anxious" group anxious about this specific problem, or for unrelated reasons?
  • Would showing people an honest account of their impact bring more people in, or discourage them?
  • Does distrust hold at the local level too, where the feedback loop is naturally faster?

Case studies

  • vTaiwan works well on contained, technical issues — would the same approach hold up on bigger, more divisive fights?
  • Does Porto Alegre-style budgeting work anywhere, or was its success tied to one specific political moment?
  • When communities like this succeed, does the benefit last — or fade once the initial excitement wears off?
  • Has making impact visible ever backfired — made people more discouraged once they could see clearly they hadn't won?
Open Questions

The Assumptions Behind This Pitch

We built this case on real evidence — but also on a few bets we haven't proven yet.

1

There's a real audience: we're assuming there are people who'd get involved if they simply knew how — not people who've already decided they don't care.

2

Engagement is worth encouraging: we're assuming civic engagement is genuinely good — that more participation leads to better outcomes, not just more noise.

3

Visible results won't backfire: we're assuming clear feedback on impact helps people build the habit of showing up. It might instead teach people to only act when a payoff is guaranteed — and tune out everything else.

How to read a Frame Creation map

This is a problem situation mapped using Kees Dorst's Frame Creation method (from Frame Innovation, MIT Press). The evidence trail runs from raw observations up to the situation itself:

  1. Signals — raw field observations, unedited.
  2. Evidence — two or more signals triangulating on one claim.
  3. Patterns — repeated behaviour visible across several pieces of evidence.
  4. Themes — what the patterns jointly reveal about the underlying condition.
  5. Problem situation — the open, complex, networked condition a new frame comes from.

The job at this stage is to investigate the field, not to solve anything. Solutions come later, and only after the field is mapped and the archaeology is deep.

Why this problem is "open," not "bounded"

A bounded problem has a known solution path. This one doesn't — it's complex (several competing explanations), networked (no actor moves alone), and dynamic (measuring responsiveness changes the behaviour of everyone being measured). Every promising case study we found relocated the tension rather than eliminating it.

What comes next in the method

This situation is mapped but not solved — and solving it is not the next step. The next steps in Frame Creation are:

  1. Map the field — identify every player connected to this situation or a future resolution. Define each by their practices and currency, not their title. Look especially for players who share the values our themes surfaced — even in distant, unexpected domains.
  2. Identify a client — from the potential problem owners, who could own this situation or adopt a resolution? What would they need to see?
  3. Choose a delivery context — through what kind of vehicle does a resolution reach the field? (An internal team, a government program, an open-source project, a startup, a nonprofit, a civic institution, a co-op — there is no right answer, only trade-offs.)
  4. Deepen the archaeology — the gaps above name what's still thin. Go find it: interview people, run deep-research sweeps, pull secondary sources.
  5. Create frames — only after the field is mapped and the archaeology is deep. "If this situation is approached as if it is ___, then ___."
Honest limits of this map

Where the evidence is thin, and we know it:

  • Card coverage is uneven. Two themes are fully built out; several patterns and evidence cards sit unattached, waiting for more signals before they climb the ladder.
  • Some cards are still untitled. They hold real signals but haven't yet had their claim named — they're shown here rather than deleted.
  • Most signals are U.S.-based. We can't yet say whether this pattern is inherent to mass democracy or an artifact of specific U.S. institutional design.
  • Single sources dressed as triangulation. A few evidence cards lean on two signals from a similar vantage point. Read those critically.
  • No decision-maker voice yet. We've heard from residents and observers, but not enough from the legislative staff and local officials who sit on the other side of the loop.
