## 1. Effort vs. Impact

Some people would engage — if they believed a given action was worth the effort. The barriers are too high and the incentives are too low: it takes real effort just to find out what's available and determine whether it matters — and when the feedback loop is broken, people stop trying because they never see results of their effort. If I go to a protest, what actually happens?

How might we make it easy for people to see what civic action is available, what it would take, and what actually happened — so showing up starts to feel worth it?

**The situation**

This covers civic engagement broadly, from national voting to local board meetings — but the sharpest opportunity is local, where feedback loops are naturally shorter and easier to fix. Approaches like Taiwan's vTaiwan (digital consensus platforms), Porto Alegre's participatory budgeting, and deliberative polling have all shown that closing the loop between action and impact is possible — none of it has been applied broadly to everyday local civic actions like ours. We need political scientists who study collective action and trust, local civic leaders and election officials who feel this gap firsthand, and people with marketing or communications skills who can help close the reach gap civic leaders can't close alone.

## 2. Frame Hypothesis

*Upskilling Labs · Closing the Loop · Assignment 2 — Effort vs. Impact*

**Frame Hypothesis**

> *If the problem is silence after input, build a visible answer — not a bigger microphone.*

### Which paradox

**Input without answer.** A public board needs open participation to be legitimate, but unstructured participation with no public reply can erode the legitimacy it is meant to create. This is the pod's Effort vs. Impact paradox at the scale of one local meeting: when people cannot see whether their effort counted, opting out is rational — and each quiet exit makes the next person's effort even less likely to matter.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Value** | Public comment exists to make local decisions legitimate. A board invites people to speak so the process is open and residents have a real chance to be heard. |
| **Failure** | That same invitation — unstructured testimony with no visible reply — produces the opposite of legitimacy. The more people speak into silence, the more clearly the institution shows that participation may not matter. |
| **Trap** | The obvious fix is more outreach: more meetings, easier forms, bigger turnout. That pours more voices into the same silent system. Decide faster with fewer speakers and you restore action, but you destroy the openness that made the meeting democratic. |

### The reframe

*Everyone assumes this is a problem about getting more people to participate. What if it's actually a problem about whether an institution can make listening visible?*

This shift moves the failure point from resident motivation to institutional response design. What becomes visible is who owns the reply, when it is due, what was heard in aggregate, and whether the answer was yes, no, or not yet. What stops mattering is raw comment volume, longer meetings, and "more civic engagement" as a goal in itself. A smaller closed loop on one agenda item is a better hackathon test than a bigger open mic.

### The stakeholder

Chair of a D.C. Advisory Neighborhood Commission (ANC). Every commissioner has a public email, and the chair already runs the meeting where neighbors speak. They want people to keep coming and to feel the commission is worth a Tuesday night. They are frustrated that after they take testimony, the only public artifact is minutes almost nobody reads — so the same neighbors either stop showing up or repeat the same comment next month. This frame lands because it does not ask them for a citywide platform or new statute. It asks them to adopt one item, name who owns the reply before comment opens, post a short "what we heard," and send a dated yes / no / not yet. That is work they can try at the next regularly scheduled meeting.

### The testability check

**Landing-page headline:** *You spoke. Here's what the board heard — and what it decided.*

## 3. Individual Presentation

**Slide 1 — One Board, Closed Loop**

Research focus: Governments elsewhere have closed the loop between public input and a public answer. What's the smallest version of that one local board could run?

| Metric | Description |
| --- | --- |
| 10–15 | Ideas Reykjavik's council addresses monthly, via Better Reykjavik (Iceland) |
| 20 days | Birmingham City Council's promised written-response window for public comments |
| 3–6 mo. | Time from idea to a public yes/no on Better Reykjavik — outcome always announced |

**Mechanisms to borrow → Smallest version for one board**

- **Transparent issue framing.** vTaiwan, Taiwan's online consultation platform, names the issue up front; one agency replies first. → **Adopt-an-item.** Pick one agenda item per meeting and name who owns the reply before comment opens.
- **Visible aggregation, not just testimony.** Pol.is, a clustering tool, does this openly — Cidade Democrática runs it in Brazil. → **A 48-hour tally, not a transcript.** Post a one-page "what we heard" summary within two days — a low-tech Pol.is.
- **A response on a stated clock.** vTaiwan and the UK's "You Said, We Did" model close the loop with a public yes, no, or not yet. → **A dated promise, personally delivered.** Answer within 10–20 days (Newport, Birmingham) and notify every commenter.

*Sources: Citizens Foundation · Council of Europe · Crowdlaw for Congress (vTaiwan) · Cidade Democrática · Birmingham City Council · Newport City Council · Participatory Budgeting Project*

### Core Problem Frame
This project is about civic participation failure caused by input without answer. The working belief is that institutions often invite people to speak, but do not provide a visible response showing what was heard, who owns the reply, when a response is due, or what decision followed.

Do not frame this primarily as a turnout or engagement problem. Frame it as an institutional response-design problem.

**Key paradox:**
- Public comment is intended to create legitimacy.
- Unstructured testimony with no visible reply can produce the opposite: people learn that participation may not matter.
- Increasing outreach without changing response design only pours more voices into the same silent system.

**Anchor line:**
> If the problem is silence after input, build a visible answer — not a bigger microphone.

### Reframe
The central reframe is:
- This is not mainly about getting more people to participate.
- It is about whether an institution can make listening visible.

Design and product work should make these elements explicit:
- Who owns the reply.
- When the reply is due.
- What was heard in aggregate.
- Whether the answer is yes, no, or not yet.

Raw comment volume, longer meetings, and generic "more engagement" goals are secondary. A smaller, closed-loop pilot on one agenda item is preferable to a larger open-ended participation channel.

### Primary Stakeholder
Default stakeholder: Chair of a D.C. Advisory Neighborhood Commission (ANC).

**Why this stakeholder matters:**
- They already run the public meeting where testimony happens.
- They want residents to keep participating and to believe the commission is worth their time.
- Today, the main artifact after testimony is often meeting minutes that few people read.
- That dynamic causes residents either to disengage or to repeat the same comment at future meetings.

**Practical constraint:**
- Favor solutions that can be tried at the next regularly scheduled meeting.
- Avoid requiring a citywide platform, statutory change, or heavy process transformation.

### Prototype / Pilot Logic
Good pilot behavior:
- Pick one agenda item.
- Name the person who owns the reply before comment opens.
- Publish a short "what we heard."
- Send or post a dated decision/status response: yes / no / not yet.

The test should be small, concrete, and behavior-changing, not a broad engagement platform.

### Product Guidance for Claude Code
When generating concepts, specs, UX, copy, or prototypes for this project:
- Optimize for visibility of response, not volume of input.
- Make accountability legible in the interface.
- Prefer lightweight workflows that fit real public meeting operations.
- Show status, ownership, timestamps, and decision trails clearly.
- Treat trust, legitimacy, and perceived effort-to-impact ratio as key outcomes.
- Avoid "engagement theater" patterns such as collecting comments without downstream acknowledgment.
- Prefer plain language over civic-tech jargon.

### Useful Experience Principles
- People need confirmation that their effort counted.
- The response loop should be public enough to build trust, but simple enough to maintain.
- A partial answer is better than silence when it is explicit and time-bound.
- Repetition in future meetings may be a symptom of invisible listening, not low-quality participation.

### Working Headline
You spoke. Here's what the board heard — and what it decided.

### Deliverable Bias
Good deliverables for this workspace include:
- product briefs
- pilot specs
- service blueprints
- meeting workflow artifacts
- decision-status templates
- public-facing response dashboards or summaries
- stakeholder interview guides
- experiment plans and success metrics

### Avoid
- Treating success as comment volume alone.
- Assuming more outreach solves the core problem.
- Large speculative platforms without a near-term operating model.
- Solutions that create more process burden than a local board can realistically carry.
