* Paradox: local election turnout falls to under 20% even though local races (schools, zoning, courts) affect daily life more directly than the presidential races that pull 69%
* The reframe sentence, completed: "Everyone assumes this is a problem about voters not caring enough to research local candidates. What if it's actually a problem about down-ballot candidates having no way to become findable at all?" — The research already points here without naming it. Look closely at the four "where the effort goes" barriers on the original slide, and two of them are not voter behaviors at all, they're candidate-side gaps. "Many down-ballot candidates have no website, no party label, and no funding to build one." "Candidates' track records are hard to locate and verify." Neither of those sentences describes something a voter is failing to do. Both describe something a candidate has failed to provide. That distinction matters, because it relocates the problem from a motivation deficit on the demand side (voters who won't bother) to an infrastructure deficit on the supply side (candidates who have nothing to offer even a voter who is actively trying).
* This reframe also explains the "where it's been overcome" column better than a voter-apathy frame does. Turnout in the Board of Education races didn't rise because voters suddenly started caring more in the abstract. It rose after a third party, in this case Frederick-area advocacy groups, ran structured candidate surveys and published the answers, including which candidates declined to respond. That is an intervention on the supply side: someone built the information layer that individual candidates couldn't build for themselves, and turnout followed. If the barrier were really about voter motivation, you would expect motivational messaging to be the thing that worked. Instead, the thing that worked was giving voters something concrete to find.
* Under this frame, what becomes visible is that the fix already has a proven shape. It is not hypothetical. Someone, somewhere, has already assembled and centralized candidate information once, in one county, for one type of race, and turnout moved. The open question isn't whether this kind of intervention works, it's how to make it cheap and repeatable enough that it doesn't depend on one well-organized local advocacy group having the capacity to run it by hand every cycle.
